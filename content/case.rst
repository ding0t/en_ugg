.. _case:

Case
====

Summary
-------
In Koiné Greek, the ``case`` ending of a word indicates how that term functions within the sentence.

Article
-------

Koine Greek is an inflected language.  In practical terms this means that in Greek,
the addition of a suffix to the root word will change it’s function within the sentence.
Koiné Greek uses various kinds of word endings. 

  These word endings (or suffixes) are referred to as “the case ending or case endings” and
  instead of being called “suffix endings” they are called “case endings.”  
  
  As a result, there is much flexibility with regard to word order in a sentence.
  It does not matter if the subject of the sentence comes first, of if the verb comes first,
  or if the object of the sentence comes first because the case of the word will determine
  its function within the sentence.  Yet there are some general patterns that are generally true.
  Those patterns will be introduced below in the section “Order of Words.”


The term "case" in Koiné refers to one of these varieties of word endings.
Case endings in Koiné Greek indicate the function of a word in a sentence.
Nouns, adjectives, pronouns, participles, and definite articles all require case endings.
Verbs and adverbs do not require a case ending.

There are five different case endings in Koiné Greek: nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, and vocative.

Generally, the :ref:`Nominative<case_nominative>` case indicates the subject of a sentence and other words that describe the subject, such as a predicate nominative or predicate adjective.
It is also used for adjectives and participles modifying the subject of the sentence.

Generally, the :ref:`Genitive<case_genitive>` case indicates possession.
  However, the genitive can also be used to indicate many other kinds of relationships such as:
          a marital relationship,
          or a relationship to a certain place or time, or
          of separation between two entities in a sentence, etc.
Some prepositions take their complement in the genitive case.

Generally, the :ref:`Dative<case_dative>` case indicates the indirect object of a verb.
However, the dative case is also commonly used to express location, means, or agency.
Some prepositions take their object in the dative case.

Generally, the :ref:`Accusative<case_accusative>` case indicates the Direct Object of a verb.  
However, the accusative case is also commonly used to express the object of an `Infinitive <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/mood_infinitive.html>`_ 
Some prepositions take their object in the accusative case.

The :ref:`Vocative<case_vocative>` case indicates the addressee of a speech, that is, the person being spoken to.

Form
~~~~

The case of a noun, pronoun, adjective, article, or participle is indicated by the case ending (or suffix) attached to the end of the word.   
These changes follow three basic patterns called *"First declension,"* *"Second declension,”* and *“Third declension.”*

First declension nouns have a feminine gender for the most part and in the nominative singular end in an alpha or an eta.  
See `First Declension <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/paradigms.html#first-declension>`_ .  A few masculine nouns end in an  -ας or –ης.  These words 
also follow a first declension pattern.

Second declension nouns end in –ος or –ον in the nominative singular.  Almost all words ending in –ος in the nominative singular are
masculine gender.  All words that end in –ον in the nominative singular are neuter gender.  
See `Second Declension <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/paradigms.html#n-4-second-declension>`_ .

Third declension nouns have stems that end in a consonant or the vowels (ι, υ, ευ).  They are made up of all three genders. 
See `Third Declension <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/paradigms.html#third-declension>`_ .

Adjectives and pronouns agree in case and number with the nouns they are modifying (or to which they are referring). Sometimes, 
the changes may be more significant.  [for example the first person singular pronoun  **ἐγώ** in the nominative case changes to **μου** 
when it is in the genitive case]   See `Adjective_paradigm <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/paradigms.html#adjectives>`_  or  
`Pronoun_paradigm <https://ugg.readthedocs.io/en/latest/paradigms.html#pronouns>`_ .

Order of Words
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Because the Greek language has a case system, the order of the words in a sentence can be changed to place an emphasis on a particular word
or clause.   The normal word order is **conjunction – verb – subject – object**.   When this order is changed there is probably a slight
emphasis being made on the words that are moved forward.  

.. csv-table::  Example: Satan tempting Jesus

  Matthew 4:3
  Εἰ,υἱὸς,εἶ,τοῦ,θεοῦ,εἰπὲ,ἵνα,οἱ,λίθοι,οὗτοι,ἄρτοι,γένωνται.
  Ei,huios,ei,tou,theou,eipe,hina,hoi,lithoi,houtoi,artoi,genōntai.
  If,son,you are,of,God,order,that,the,stones,these,bread,they become.

**If you are the son of God**, *order that these stones become bread.*

Note:  In this sentence the phrase “if you are the son of god” has been moved to the first position in the sentence.   The movement of the
phrase to the front of the sentence places a slight emphasis or stress on the condition.
